Unhosted wallets and the Travel Rule: proportionate controls

Mukesh Kumar, CAMS · Virtual assets · November 2026

Self-custody is a normal way to hold virtual assets. Most transfers between an exchange and a customer's own wallet are exactly what they appear to be. The compliance task is to separate that ordinary activity from the small share of transfers that carry real exposure, without burying the customer, or the investigations team, in friction.

Start with what the wallet has touched

Blockchain analytics shows an address's direct and indirect exposure: whether funds came from, or are heading to, mixers, darknet markets, sanctioned addresses, scam clusters or high-risk services. That exposure is a better first filter than the bare fact that a wallet is unhosted. The controls I find useful are:

  • Screening every deposit and withdrawal address at the time of the transfer, not only during periodic review.
  • Separate thresholds for direct and indirect exposure, because a wallet two hops from a mixer is a different proposition from one funded by it directly.
  • Clear rules for when a hit pauses a transfer, when it creates an alert for review, and when it goes straight to the Principal Officer.

Applying the Travel Rule to self-hosted transfers

The Travel Rule requires originator and beneficiary information to accompany virtual asset transfers between service providers. When the counterparty is an unhosted wallet there is no other service provider to exchange that data with, so the work shifts to knowing who controls the wallet. Proportionate steps include collecting the counterparty's details from the customer, confirming ownership for transfers above an internal threshold, and treating repeated transfers to many unrelated unhosted wallets as a pattern worth reviewing in its own right.

Where the risk usually shows

The patterns that deserve escalation are rarely about one wallet. They show up as behaviour: fiat deposited and moved out to several private wallets within days, leaving little behind; inflows from many wallets consolidated and sent on almost immediately; or a new account receiving substantial value from private wallets before any trading. Monitoring scenarios written around those sequences catch more than rules written around any single address.

The aim is a control set a regulator can follow: each threshold has a stated rationale, each escalation route is documented, and each decision on an alert is recorded. That is what makes a risk-based approach defensible rather than discretionary.
